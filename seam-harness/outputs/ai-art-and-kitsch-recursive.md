Kitsch by Default: AI Art and the Path of Least Resistance

By kitsch I mean something specific, and I should say so plainly at the start, because the word usually arrives with the verdict already inside it. Kitsch is art-like output that delivers feeling already digested, through formulas already proven, flattering the taste we already have, and producing effect without effort on either side of the transaction. Not every one of those marks has to be present; together they form a recognizable cluster. And notice what the definition does not mention: how the thing was made. Each mark can be tested on the finished work — a sunset over lavender, a weeping child with enormous eyes — without knowing whether a hand, a camera, or a model produced it.

That last point matters because the case against AI art is stronger than its defenders like to admit. A model trained on the archive of images people have already approved learns, almost by definition, what approval looks like. Steered by a short prompt and a round of cherry-picking, it tends back toward the mean of that archive — the composition that reads instantly, the mood nobody has to work for. And where platforms reward the immediately appealing, the path of least resistance runs well lit and downhill. You can see where it leads: the now-familiar flood of glossy, symmetrical, faintly devotional images — waxy skin, glassy light, sentiment turned up like a dial. The details will change as the tools improve; the pull will not. Kitsch is what AI image-making does when nobody pushes back.

But a default is not a destiny, and a tool's bias is not a verdict on every use of it. Sentimental formula long predates machine generation: the greeting-card verse, the calendar landscape, the salon canvas engineered to reassure. AI did not invent that economy of feeling; it made its products cheaper and faster. The proper target of complaint, then as now, is the sentiment and the market that rewards it, not the instrument. History offers a caution here, though only a modest one. Photography, cheap color printing, and recorded sound were each dismissed in turn as mechanical substitutes for art, and each later produced work nobody now doubts is art. That proves nothing about where AI will end up; it does shift the burden of proof onto anyone who claims the medium is inherently kitsch.

What settles the question is not the tool but the use. The same image can be kitsch in one setting — mass-printed to soothe — and genuine commentary in another, redeployed deadpan by someone who knows exactly what they're doing. Kitsch is a judgment about intention and reception, and the judgment has a discriminating edge a viewer can actually feel. The AI work that escapes kitsch is the one where a maker has selected against the obvious — kept the jarring detail the average would smooth away, pushed the model past what it offers willingly, taken responsibility for a strangeness that doesn't resolve on first look. The work that remains kitsch is accepted precisely because nothing in it resists: the feeling arrives pre-approved, the beauty costs nothing, the profundity evaporates by morning.

This means the honest complaint about most AI imagery is really a complaint about a habit. The flood exists because frictionless sentiment is what the workflow hands you when you ask for nothing more. But friction can be re-imposed by anyone willing to slow down, reject the first hundred easy answers, and stand behind the hundred-and-first. The question "is AI art kitsch?" has no single answer, and that's the point: it has as many answers as there are uses. The machine offers the mean; what a person does with the offer is the art, or the kitsch.
