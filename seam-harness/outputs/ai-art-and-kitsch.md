Kitsch, at Scale

You know kitsch before you have a word for it: the seascape in a thousand motel rooms, waves frozen mid-curl, a sunset tuned to gold no sky ever held; the big-eyed child on the greeting card, clutching a puppy, asking a tenderness that costs you nothing. Kitsch is not simply bad taste; it is feeling that arrives pre-digested, the work done on your behalf—nothing risked, nothing ambiguous, nothing asked. You are not looking at the sea; you are being handed the idea of it, shrink-wrapped. The pleasure is genuine, and that is the problem: pleasure without exposure, sentiment insured against surprise.

Now scroll any social feed and meet kitsch's new house style: the AI portrait. You have seen it—the glossy, cinematic likeness, skin lit like poured honey, everyone rendered as the movie poster of themselves, flattering and strangely interchangeable, as if one agreeable painter had done them all. In a way, one has. Image generators are trained on vast corpora of existing pictures; what they learn is, in effect, the average of how we have pictured the world. You prompt the way you make a wish—a line of text, a hope—and no author stands behind the result risking anything on it. Each image costs almost nothing, so supply is limitless; we do the rest, selecting and circulating the ones that please us most.

That is why AI is not merely capable of kitsch but is its most efficient engine yet. Kitsch was always a business of averaging—finding the feeling the most people already want, edges sanded off. The old economy still needed someone to paint the seascape, to guess the public mood and gamble on the guess. The new system automates the guess. The corpus is the average; the prompt is the wish; failure costs nothing, so nothing is ventured. Reassurance, once manufactured, is now manufactured at scale.

We have heard a version of this before: photography was damned as mechanical soullessness and went on to teach us new ways of seeing. The rhyme is real, but the difference matters. The camera caught one scene at a time and charged for every frame; the generator averages everything and charges nothing per image. Corpus-averaging and limitless zero-cost supply are genuinely new, and they change what the machine is for.

Can AI art escape kitsch? Yes, and the conditions can be stated. It escapes when a person uses the tool to court failure instead of avoiding it—prompting against the grain, keeping the accidents the average would have ironed out, letting the portrait stay half a stranger. It escapes when the work sustains ambiguity, withholding the easy resolution instead of delivering it on contact. And it escapes when the averaging itself becomes the subject—when the image lets you watch the smoothing happen, the flattery assembling itself, the ghost of a million prior pictures made visible. The test travels: faced with any new image, ask where the risk is and who bears it; ask whether the feeling resolves on the spot or lingers and complicates. If the smoothing is used to smooth, it is kitsch; if it is resisted, broken, or exposed, it is art.

By that standard, most AI images now flooding the feeds are kitsch—not because a machine made them, but because of what the machine is being asked to do: manufacture reassurance, endlessly, for almost nothing. That is the verdict, and its condition is use. Kitsch never required insincerity, only the absence of risk; an engine that averages everything and dares nothing is that absence, industrialized. The safe wish is now the cheapest commodity on earth. Art will come from this tool where art always comes from: someone willing to wish dangerously.
